Denny Kopp , Michael Hassoun ,A d a rK a l i r, and Lars Mönch
Abstract—We present a new set of simulation models, orga-
nized in a testbed. The aim of the testbed consists in pro-
viding researchers with a platform able to credibly represent
the complexity of modern semiconductor manufacturing. The
testbed is open to public use, and include so far four mod-
els. A high-volume/low-mix model and a low-volume/high-mix
model constitutes the foundation of the testbed. Two additional
models incorporate the complexity of engineering lots. We con-
clude this paper by presenting a case study that demonstrates
that the third and fourth model can be used to assess the
performance of integrated dispatching strategies for production
and engineering lots.
Index Terms—Discrete-event simulation, semiconductor man-
ufacturing, testbed, production planning and control, simulation
experiments.

together under a testing platform often referred as a testbed.
A testbed also includes a documentation of the features of the
different datasets together with some benchmark results for

II. P
ROBLEM ANALYSIS AND RELATED WORK
A. Requirements for a State-of-the-Art Simulation Testbed
We brieﬂy summarize the main functional requirements for
the SMT2020 testbed based on [8], [9]:
1. Support of Different Simulation Model Types: The
testbed has to include simulation models of low-
volume/high-mix (LV/HM) and high-volume/low-mix
(HV/LM) wafer fabs. The LV/HM setting is used to
represent make-to-order (MTO) environments such as
foundries, producing either logic or memory devices.
The corresponding models have to contain at least ten
products. Due dates are required for all lots, the moving
entities in wafer fabs. A lot consists of wafers, thin
silicon discs. In the HV/LM setting, two high-volume
products should be modeled to represent dedicated
fabs, typically producing logic devices in a make-
to-stock (MTS) environment. The number of process
steps per route should be more than 500 to reﬂect
the complexity of modern wafer fabs [1]. More than
100 tool groups, i.e., work centers, have to be modeled
in each simulation model for the same reason. Moreover,
given the importance of engineering activities in wafer
fabs [10], SMT2020 has to include simulation models
that incorporate engineering activities.
2. Availability Modeling: Scheduled and unscheduled
downtimes should be represented in all simulations mod-
els of SMT2020 since preventative maintenance (PM)
actions and tool breakdowns are typical for the compli-
cated machinery of wafer fabs [1].
3. Machine Rate Modeling: Batch processing machines, i.e.
tools where several lots are processed at the same time
on a single tool [1] and cascading tools [11] have to be
represented in the models to mimic important process
features. Load and unload times on the tools and trans-
portation times between tool groups have to be modeled.
Moreover, setups, most importantly sequence-dependent
setups which are often longer than the pure process-
ing time [1], [2], have to be modeled in some detail to
represent the offered capacity in a correct way.
4. Functional Area Speciﬁc Complexities: Important fea-
tures of certain functional areas have to be represented
in SMT2020 models. Such features are, for instance,
lot-to-lens dedications for steppers in the photolithogra-
phy area, sampling rates for metrology tools, and critical
queue time (CQT) constraints for the dry etch area.
5. Operational Complexities: High priority lots, so-called
hot lots, are known to have a disruptive impact on the
work-in-process (WIP) ﬂow [12], and as such, are to
be represented in all SMT2020 models. It is important
to represent engineering lots in some simulation models
of the testbed due to the fact that up to one third of
all WIP lots in a wafer fab can belong to engineering
activities [10]. CQT process segments [13] are another
disruptive feature of many modern fabs and these have
been incorporated at least in some of the SMT2020 mod-
els. In contrast to the MIMAC datasets [5], [14], there is
no need to model operators given the degree of automa-
tion with respect to loading, unloading, and lot transport
in modern wafer fabs.
We identify two additional non-functional requirements
related to the usage of the SMT2020 testbed:
1. Representation of the Simulation Models of the
Testbed: The simulation models belonging to the
SMT2020 testbed should be represented in such a way
that potential users can implement them without relying
on a proprietary simulation tool. ASCII or Excel ﬁles
with a format similar to the MIMAC datasets [14] and
their derivatives [15] are preferable.
2. Providing Performance Measure Values of the
Simulation Models: The values of common performance
measures in wafer fabs including cycle time (CT), avail-
ability per functional area, tool utilization, bottlenecks,
and operating curves must be reported to provide
potential users with a way to check whether their
implementation of a simulation model works as
intended or not.
B. Discussion of Related Work
Discrete-event simulation is an important tool for decision-
making in wafer fabs [1]. There is some evidence that
building a full wafer fab simulation model from scratch is
time-consuming and error-prone [2]. Therefore, researchers in
semiconductor manufacturing are interested in applying sim-
ulation models of testbeds. The following four simulation
testbeds are fairly often applied by researchers.

Each wafer fab model includes up to 260 machines

for CT prediction in wafer fabs. Engineering lots are
added to dataset 1 in [28] to assess integrated planning
formulations for both production and engineering lots in
a rolling horizon setting.
2. The MiniFab model proposed by Spier and Kempf
in [29] is a reduced-scale simulation model that mimics
the typical behavior of a wafer fab. It contains reentrant
process ﬂows, batch processes, and sequence-dependent
setup times. Using this model, problem instances for
evaluating a full wafer fab scheduling approach are
derived in [30]. An integrated scheduling and auto-
mated material handling approach is assessed in [31] in
a rolling horizon setting using the MiniFab model.
3. A scaled-down representation of a real-world wafer fab
at Harris Semiconductor is described in [32]. It is used
by Kacar and Uzsoy [33] to estimate clearing functions
using simulation-based optimization. Production plan-
ning approaches for wafer fabs are assessed in a rolling
horizon setting using this model in [34], [35].
4. A generic 300 mm model including automated material
handing features is proposed by SEMATECH in [36].
It is used, for instance, in [37] to generate problem
instances for global fab-wide scheduling approaches.
This model is less frequently applied in comparison to
the three other testbeds and simulation models described
before.

III. SMT2020 F
EATURES
We provide details on the various SMT2020 models avail-
able at this stage. Building baseline models do not require
them to be precisely matching any speciﬁc fab, just to pro-
vide the community a baseline that makes sense and is widely
accepted. During the preparation of this paper, feedback from
the community was collected to ensure as much as possible
that reasonable features are chosen for the datasets and that
they are parameterized in an appropriate way.
The models are designed and tested under steady state.
Speciﬁc transient scenarios are left to the users to design
and run to answer their speciﬁc needs. In general, the mod-
els presented in SMT2020 can be seen as an update on the
MIMAC datasets. The main differences between these two
generations of testbeds are the following:
• The larger size (number of tools and steps) proposed in
SMT2020 better ﬁts current fab scale.
• SMT2020 provides a detailed modelling of scheduled and
unscheduled downtime instead of a uniﬁed distribution
for stoppage and repair times.
• Hot Lots and engineering lots are represented.
• Complex operational features that were irrelevant or
non-existent at the time of the MIMAC creation are
represented:
- lot-to-Lens dedication
- cascading
- critical queue times.

The different datasets in SMT2020 are realistic in their
scale. Depending on the models, close to a thousand machines
of 105 types are modeled. Identical machines share all their

Ten products with route lengths ranging from 242 to

500 steps) and the rest are shorter. The maximum number of
layers is 44. These routes share some of their operations with
other routes. Datasets 1 and 3 (HV/LM) are based on two

4 (LV/HM) make use of all ten products. Datasets 3 and 4 add
engineering lots to datasets 1 and 2, respectively.
The production rates are deﬁned for each tool type based
on a processing time for a single wafer, a lot of 25 wafers,
or for a batch. Batches are used in the diffusion area and are
groups of lots that are processed at the same time on a single
diffusion tube. The processing time is sampled from a contin-
uous uniform distribution ( +−5% around the nominal value)
in each simulation run. A ﬁxed loading and unloading time
is added to the processing time for the lot or batch. The time
required to transfer lots between tool groups is represented by
a delay uniformly sampled between 5 to 10 minutes.
In all SMT2020 datasets, the maximum batch size is set
between 100 and 150 wafers with an allowance of one lot to
allow for some ﬂexibility in the operation. For example, if
the maximum batch size is 6 lots (150 wafers), a batch can
be processed as soon as 5 lots are found waiting in the tool
group queue.
A special regime, denoted as cascading, takes place in cer-
tain tools that allow different parts, i.e., wafers or lots, to
undergo part of their process in parallel with others. This is
illustrated in Figure 1 for the simplest case of a two-stage pro-
cess. Practically, this means that the time required for the ﬁrst
process unit (wafer or lot) of a group is longer than for the
remainder. It also means that loading and unloading phases are
only accounted for once, at the beginning and end of the cas-
cade. The remaining loading/unloading operations take place
in parallel with processing of other lots and therefore do not
contribute to the processing time. All tools in implant, pla-
narization, thin ﬁlms and wet etch areas, and about half of the
tools in dielectric area operate with cascading.
When setups apply, a setup avoidance function is added to
the regular prioritization scheme that forces the tool to remain

avoid having what is called in the fab jargon “one of a kind”
tools, meaning that a minimum number of two machines was
set to ensure a minimum tool redundancy.
B. Availability Modeling
The level of detail in the availability modeling sets the
models in SMT2020 apart from their predecessors that tend
to model all stoppages as a set of distributions for the time
between failures and the time to repair. In SMT2020 PM, also
known as scheduled down time (SDT) and breakdowns, i.e.,
unscheduled down time (UDT), are modeled separately. For
each area, UDT and SDT are carefully balanced. In areas
known for their stability like metrology or planarization, we
aim at 90% of the total down time accounted by SDT and
only 10% by UDT, while in lithography, where reliability is
an issue, the target for SDT is set at 50% of the total down
time only.
1) UDT: Under the UDT are found a wide range of events
causing production to stop unexpectedly: First and foremost,
equipment malfunction, but also SPC out-of-control, either
recipe-speciﬁc or related to defectivity, delays or breakdowns
in ancillary or transport systems, shortages of replacement
parts required in troubleshooting either in the fab or from the
supplier etc. Although other distributions are sometimes better
suited to precisely render the TBF and TTR distribution, the
exponential distribution offers a reasonable baseline (see [39]),
and is simple to implement. This is why we use an exponential
distribution to sample both time before failure (TBF) and time
to repair (TTR). The mean time before failure (MTBF) is set

C. Metrology and Quality Modeling
The frequency of inspection on metrology tools is deﬁned
by a sampling rate set individually for each operation. All lots
are inspected (100% rate) for approximately half of lithogra-
phy metrology operations, the other half is characterized by
measurement rates between 10% and 50%. Thin ﬁlms metrol-
ogy operations have inspection rates ranging from 30% to
40%. Typically, the process front-end where the transistors are
built is more sensitive to particle contamination than the back-
end where the metal layers are formed. This is why defectivity
metrology uses a sampling rate set between 50% and 60% in
the front-end and 10% in the back-end. In some lithography
operations (about one third) 0.5% to 2% of lots are reworked.
Rework involves a re-doing of the process segment imme-
diately preceding the rework operation, following the same
recipes and on the same machines.
D. Constraints
To cope with setups, operators tend to run long series of
lots of the same operation on a machine before switching to
another. This mode of operation is one of the major sources of
variability that plagues WIP ﬂow in fabs. Setups are therefore
a critical feature of the proposed models, and are incorporated
in three areas. Two tool groups in the lithography area undergo
setups for reticle change. In the back-end of the process two
dry etch tool groups have simple sequence-dependent setups
(two groups of operations). In the implant area, setups are
required for gas change, and so three setup groups are deﬁned.
In lithography, a lot-to-lens dedication scheme is applied on
three different tool groups, and for three groups of operations.
The sensibility to overlay issues is a characteristic of modern
processes as opposed to older ones. Depending on the process,
the machinery, and the product value, companies differ in their
way to tackle this topic. They may simply accept the yield
loss if the value is low and the time to market is critical (typi-
cally for memories) or try to balance between operational and
quality concerns either by running critical steps under a strict
Lot-to-Lens dedication scheme or by allowing some kind of
tool grouping based on their optical characteristics, allowing
lots to be processed indistinctively within said groups. In our
models, when a lot-to-lens dedication applies, once a lot is
processed on a speciﬁc tool for the ﬁrst of these operations, it
must be returned to the same tool for the remaining operations
in the group.
CQTs are deﬁned and provided for the datasets, but no
special dispatching rule is applied at the entrance of a CQT
segment at this point.
E. Speciﬁcs of SMT2020 Models
1) Preliminary Steps: One of the key metrics of a fab
behavior is CT. Since it is product-dependent, and in order
to be able to present aggregated results, we normalize each
product’s CT by its minimum achievable value, denoted as
raw processing time (RPT). The result is what is known as
ﬂow factor (FF) [1], calculated as the ratio of the product
CT and its RPT. As a preliminary step, we ﬁrst run a model
with lots starting at very long intervals. As a result, these
lots progress in an empty fab and their waiting time is close
to zero (the speciﬁc model is unimportant since the number
of tools has no impact on a single lot trajectory). In such
a setup, no minimum batch size is required for these lots in
order not to wait extremely long period of times at diffusion
tools. The products’ minimum CT is then measured to obtain
the RPT. The source of variability in the resultant CT lays in
the fact that some lots experience delays due to PMs or that
they are sampled in metrology steps. The resultant RPTs for
all ten products (regular and hot lots) are given in Table II.
2) Dataset 1: For this ﬁrst model which aims at represent-
ing a HV/LM fab, only routes 3 and 4 are used, whose lengths
are 583 and 343 steps, respectively. Although the capacity
of the fab is around 10,200 WSPW, the proposed working
point in order to reach a realistic utilization is 10,000 WSPW
(5,000 WSPW for each product), from which 2.5% are hot lots.
This translates to the introduction of a new regular lot of each
product every 51.69 minutes, and of a hot lot every 2,016 min-
utes. Often, high-volume manufacturing is associated with
a MTS mode. Therefore, we do not represent any due date for
the lots and adopt a simple First-Come First-Served (FCFS)
dispatching rule along the process.
3) Dataset 2: This second model aims at representing
a LV/HM fab. All ten products, with route length ranging from
242 steps for product 5 to 583 steps for product 3, are used
and launched into the fab at the same rate. Although the model
seems stable for a load as high as 10,250 WSPW, we decided
to set the working point of this model to 10,000 WSPW
(1,000 WSPW per product) to reach a realistic maximum uti-
lization. Here too, 2.5% of lots are hot lots. This translates
to the introduction of a new regular lot every 258.46 minutes
and of a hot lot every 10,080 minutes for each product.

date but also the remaining process time required to complete
production. The CR index of a lot is deﬁned as the ratio of the
time remaining to due date and the processing time required to
completion [1]. In dataset 2, lot due dates are determined as:

as a function of the fab load. In our case, in order to unify the

values. We present in Figure 2 and Figure 3 the percentiles

on a single simulation run with a length of eight years where
the ﬁrst year serves as warmup period. All lot cycle times are
collected to derive the order statistics which are required to
compute the quantiles. More advanced sequential procedures
for estimating quantiles with a given precision such as the
zoom in (ZI) procedure [40] are not used in this paper since

A basic dispatching strategy, abbreviated by BASE, is
used for all tool groups in datasets 3 and 4. Production hot
lots (PHL) and engineering hot lots (EHL) have a higher prior-
ity than production-related regular lots (PRL) and engineering-
related regular lots (ERL), i.e., hot lots are always preferred
to regular lots. Given lots with the same priority, FCFS for
dataset 3 and CR for dataset 4 are applied. The priorities for
EHL, PHL, ERL, and PRL are 20, 20, 10, and 10, respec-
tively. Moreover, integrated dispatching strategies similar to
the ones from [10] for both production lots (PLs) and engi-
neering lots (ELs) are applied. PLs and ELs consist of hot and
regular production and engineering lots, respectively. The ﬁrst
additional strategy is called engineering ﬁrst (EF), where ELs
have a higher priority than PLs, i.e., if lots of type EL are avail-
able they are always preferred. The priorities for EHL, PHL,
ERL, and PRL are chosen as 25, 20, 15, and 10, respectively.
The EF strategy is applied to all tool groups.
The capacity allocation to engineering (CAtE) strategy
only allows engineering activities within so-called engineering
intervals with the exception that if no ELs are available within
the engineering interval, production activities are allowed
and vice versa. The lengths of the engineering and produc-
tion intervals have to be chosen. Production and engineering
intervals are repeated in a consecutive manner, i.e., it is enough
to specify pairs (lp, le) where lp is the length of the produc-
tion interval and le the length of the engineering interval. The
settings (151.2, 16.8), (75.6, 8.4), and (21.6, 2.4) in hours are
used for dataset 3, whereas (134.6, 33.4), (67.2, 16.8), and
(19.2, 4.8) are the corresponding settings for the simulation
experiments with dataset 4. This leads to a periodicity of one
week, half a week, and a single day.
A tool group-speciﬁc number of lots, a so-called trigger
limit, must be prescribed for the changeover trigger (CoT)
strategy. If the number of ELs waiting in front of a particular
tool group reaches or exceeds the trigger limit, this number
of ELs will be processed. Trigger limits of 100, 50, 25, and
10 lots are applied for both datasets in the experiments.
Both CAtE and CoT are only used for dispatching on the
LithoTrack_FE_95 and LithoTrack_FE_115 tool groups which
are formed by steppers, typical bottlenecks in wafer fabs. For

VI. A CCESSING AND USING THE DATASETS
The testbed was created and tested using the AutoSched
AP simulation software. The four simulation models with the
discussed features are publicly available at [16]. We provide
access to the testbed models on the following levels:
1. The four datasets of the testbed are represented as
a set of EXCEL sheets in a generic, i.e., simulation
tool-independent manner (ﬁles SMT_20-20_Model_Da-
ta_*.xlsx in the folder General_Data of the testbed).
2. We provide a detailed documentation of
the testbed for users of Autosched AP (ﬁle
SMT_2020_AutoSched_AP_documentation.pdf in
the AutoSched folder of the testbed). The ﬁle contains
the technical information necessary to navigate and
use the data ﬁles of the simulation models. The main
features of the different datasets are described and their
implementation using AutoSched AP are explained.
Applicants of the testbed can use the simulation models
directly in AutoSched AP. They are located in the
AutoSched folder of the testbed.
3. It is also possible to use the datasets for other
discrete-event simulation tools. For instance, the sim-
ulation engine AnyLogic is popular in semiconduc-
tor manufacturing [41]. To make such attempts eas-
ier we also provide a simulation tool-independent
documentation of the datasets by means of the ﬁle
SMT_2020_generic_format.pdf which is again located
in the General_Data folder of the testbed [16].
The following procedure is recommended to use the
datasets:
1. In a ﬁrst step, the model has to be implemented using
the simulation tool of choice. Alternatively, the provided
simulation models can be directly used if the AutoSched
AP software is available.
2. Simulation runs have then to be carried out based on
the simulation models from Step 1. Statistics have to be
gathered for TH and CT for all the models, whereas the
number of ontime lots has to be additionally collected
for the LV/HM models. Simulation results can be found
in the electronic supplement for a direct comparison.
3. If the values of the performance measures of interest
obtained in Step 2 are close to the ones from the sup-
plement, the simulation models can be used to answere
speciﬁc questions. This might require to add additional
functionality to the simulation models or to couple
the simulation models with planning and control algo-
rithms. In the latter situation, the infrastructure sketched
in [15], [42] can be applied.
VII. C
ONCLUSION
Concluding a two-year long development effort, we present
the semiconductor manufacturing researchers and practitioners
the new simulation testbed SMT2020 which aims at mim-
icking the behavior of modern fabs. During this development
phase, the main ideas and preliminary results were presented
in [8], [9] to the community who was asked to provide feed-
back. We carefully incorporated the required features and
validated the result to ensure the relevance of our models.
The core of SMT2020 is composed of a set of four mod-
els, namely datasets 1 and 2 which provide the foundation
of the testbed by representing a HV/LM fab and a LV/HM
fab respectively and datasets 3 and 4 which add engineering
lots to datasets 1 and 2, respectively. SMT2020 is available
under [16], and we encourage researchers to use it when issues
relevant to modern fabs are to be studied. The characteristics
of the simulation models are reviewed herein and we presented
important performance measure values to allow comparisons
with future bodies of work.
We demonstrated the usage of the testbed datasets by per-
forming a simulation study where we assessed the performance
of integrated dispatching strategies for production and engi-
neering activities. Datasets 3 and 4 were extended by adding
customized dispatching strategies. Related performance mea-
sure values are reported.
There are several directions for future research. Consistent
with the idea of tackling managerial issues related to features
that emerged in modern fabs, our planned next steps are to
tackle the issue of CQT constraints and how to reduce their
impact on operation. As a second research avenue, we are
interested in extending the integrated planning formulations
from [43] for production and engineering activities towards
datasets 3 and 4. It is also desirable to include some of the
SMT2020 models as nodes in the semiconductor supply chain
testbed proposed in [15].
